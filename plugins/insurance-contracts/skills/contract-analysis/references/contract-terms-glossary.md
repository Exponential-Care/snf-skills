# Managed-Care Contract Terms — SNF Glossary

Definitions of contract terms as they matter to a skilled nursing facility.
Each entry has two parts: what the term means, and why an SNF administrator,
managed-care director, or biller should care. Terms are grouped by contract
area; alphabetical index at the end.

## Rates & payment methodology

### Per diem
A single all-inclusive daily rate covering a defined bundle of services
(typically room and board, nursing, and often therapy and generic
pharmacy).
**Why it matters:** "All-inclusive" is only as good as the exclusion and
carve-out lists. If a service isn't carved out or excluded, the per diem
absorbs it — the SNF bears the cost of everything the contract is silent on.

### Leveled per diem (level grid)
A per-diem structure with multiple tiers ("levels"), each with its own rate
and qualifying clinical criteria.
**Why it matters:** The grid is where the money is. Vague level criteria,
missing combination rules, and under-leveling by cautious staff are the three
biggest revenue leaks in leveled contracts. See "Level" below.

### Case rate
A single fixed payment for an entire stay (or episode), regardless of length.
**Why it matters:** The SNF takes length-of-stay risk. A case rate without a
long-stay outlier provision means one 60-day stay can consume the margin from
many routine stays. Ask what happens when the stay runs long, and whether
readmissions within a window are bundled into the original rate.

### Percent-of-Medicare
Rates defined as a percentage of what Medicare would pay (e.g., a percentage
of the PDPM rate).
**Why it matters:** Check which *year's* Medicare schedule the contract
references. "Percent of the 2022 fee schedule" is a rate cut every year that
Medicare updates and the contract doesn't. Also confirm whether the reference
includes Medicare's case-mix adjustments or a flat average.

### RUG-based / PDPM-based rates
Rates tied to Medicare's patient classification systems — RUG (Resource
Utilization Groups, the legacy system) or PDPM (Patient-Driven Payment Model,
current since 2019).
**Why it matters:** RUG-based contracts still exist and require maintaining a
classification workflow Medicare itself no longer uses. For either system,
confirm who performs the classification, on what assessment schedule, and how
disputes over the classification are resolved.

### Rate escalator
A built-in periodic rate increase — a fixed percentage, a CPI-linked
adjustment, or a scheduled reopener.
**Why it matters:** A contract with no escalator plus evergreen renewal is a
compounding real-rate cut: costs (especially labor) rise annually while the
rate doesn't. This is one of the most common and most costly silent gaps.

### Lesser-of (lesser-of billed charges)
A clause paying the lesser of the contracted rate or the facility's billed
charges.
**Why it matters:** It converts the chargemaster into a rate ceiling. If
billed charges for any line item sit below the contract rate — common when
chargemasters go years without review — the facility is paid the lower
number, silently. Before signing any lesser-of contract, audit charges
against contract rates.

### Chargemaster
The facility's master list of billed charges for services and items.
**Why it matters:** Mostly invisible in fee-for-service SNF life — until a
lesser-of clause makes it the effective cap on every payment. Keep it current
and above contracted rates.

### Usual and customary / billed charges
The facility's undiscounted charge for a service.
**Why it matters:** Appears in lesser-of clauses, in out-of-network
provisions, and as the basis some contracts use for non-covered services
billed to patients. Know which meaning a given clause is using.

### Patient responsibility (copay, coinsurance, patient liability)
The portion of the cost the member owes — copays, coinsurance, deductibles,
or (in some plans) a share-of-cost amount.
**Why it matters:** Confirm whether the payer's payment is *net* of patient
responsibility (facility must collect the difference from the patient) or
*gross* (payer pays full rate and handles member cost-share itself). Getting
this wrong misstates expected revenue per day and creates collection problems
discovered months later.

## Levels & acuity

### Level (acuity level)
A tier in a leveled per-diem contract, defined by clinical criteria (services
delivered, conditions, treatment intensity) and carrying its own daily rate.
**Why it matters:** The level, not the headline rate, determines what the SNF
is actually paid per day. Three chronic problems: (1) criteria written as
judgment calls ("complex care") that the payer's reviewer interprets
narrowly; (2) no stated combination rule — does the patient need ALL listed
criteria, ANY one, or some count?; (3) staff defaulting to lower levels
because the criteria feel risky to claim. Leveling should be an explicit,
evidence-backed determination, re-checked when the patient's condition
changes.

### Leveling (leveling a patient)
The process of matching a patient's clinical picture to the contract's level
criteria to establish the payable rate — at admission and throughout the stay.
**Why it matters:** Under-leveling is unrecovered revenue for care actually
delivered; over-leveling without documentation is a takeback risk at audit.
The defensible middle is a criterion-by-criterion determination with chart
evidence for each "met."

### Qualifying criteria
The specific clinical requirements attached to each level (e.g., "IV
medication administration," "wound vac," "isolation precautions").
**Why it matters:** Objective, service-based criteria protect the SNF;
subjective criteria hand the effective rate decision to the payer's reviewer.
In negotiation, push judgment terms toward named services and measurable
thresholds.

### Acuity
The overall intensity of a patient's care needs.
**Why it matters:** The concept behind every level grid. Contracts monetize
acuity differently — through levels, case-mix systems, or carve-outs — and
the SNF's admissions decisions depend on knowing which acuity drivers a given
contract actually pays for.

## Carve-outs, exclusions & risk limits

### Carve-out
A service or item paid separately from (usually in addition to) the per diem
— commonly high-cost drugs, dialysis, ventilator care, bariatric equipment
and care, complex wound supplies, transportation.
**Why it matters:** Carve-outs are the pressure valve that makes an
all-inclusive per diem survivable for high-cost patients. A contract with no
high-cost-drug carve-out means one admission on an expensive specialty drug
can cost more per day than the entire per diem. Confirm for each carve-out:
the trigger (threshold, diagnosis, order), the payment basis (cost, invoice
cost plus a percentage, stated rate), and whether it pays *in addition to* or
*instead of* the per diem.

### Pass-through
A carve-out paid at the facility's actual cost (often invoice cost),
"passed through" to the payer.
**Why it matters:** The cleanest protection against high-cost items — but
only if the documentation requirements (invoices, NDC codes, administration
records) are known before the first claim. Missed documentation converts a
pass-through into an absorbed cost.

### High-cost drug threshold
The dollar level (per dose, per day, or per stay) above which a drug becomes
separately payable.
**Why it matters:** The threshold's *unit* matters as much as its amount — a
per-stay threshold is far harder to reach than a per-day one. Screen every
referral's medication list against the threshold before accepting the
admission; this is where referral decisions and contract terms meet.

### Exclusion
A service the contract does not cover at all (vs. a carve-out, which is
covered but paid separately).
**Why it matters:** Exclusions define what the SNF must not bill the payer
for — and raise the question of who *does* pay. If the contract is silent on
patient billing for excluded services, that gap needs resolving before the
service is delivered, not after.

### Stop-loss (outlier provision)
A provision that changes the payment method once a stay's costs or length
exceed a threshold — e.g., converting to cost-based or discounted-charge
payment above the threshold.
**Why it matters:** Stop-loss is the SNF's insurance against catastrophic
stays under case rates and flat per diems. Its absence means unlimited
downside on a single admission. If present, know the threshold, what counts
toward it, and the notice the payer requires when a stay approaches it.

### Lesser-of

## Authorization & utilization review

### Prior authorization (initial authorization)
The payer's approval, obtained before or at admission, for the stay and
usually for a specific level and number of days.
**Why it matters:** Days delivered without required authorization are
routinely denied regardless of medical necessity. Know the contract's
required timing, the payer's turnaround commitment, and what the contract
says about weekend/after-hours admissions when the payer can't be reached.

### Concurrent review (continued-stay review)
The payer's ongoing review during the stay, approving additional days or
levels in increments.
**Why it matters:** Concurrent review cadence drives daily operational work —
someone must submit clinicals on the payer's schedule or days get denied. The
contract should state the cadence, what clinical information suffices, how
quickly the payer must respond, and the status of days that pass while a
review is pending.

### Recertification (recert)
The periodic re-approval of continued stay or continued level, on a stated
cadence (e.g., weekly).
**Why it matters:** A missed recert deadline is one of the most preventable
denial causes in SNF managed care. Build the contract's cadence into the
case-management calendar on day one of each admission.

### Retro authorization (retroactive authorization)
Authorization granted after services were delivered — for emergency
admissions, retro-eligibility, or administrative misses.
**Why it matters:** Many contracts prohibit or tightly limit retro auth. If
the contract has no retro-auth provision, an admission accepted before
coverage was known may be unpayable. This is a key negotiation ask for
facilities taking hospital discharges nights and weekends.

### Medical necessity
The payer's standard that services must be clinically required, per the
payer's criteria or guidelines.
**Why it matters:** Contracts often incorporate the payer's internal criteria
"as amended from time to time" — a moving target the SNF never signed. Ask
which criteria set applies, whether the SNF can obtain it, and whether
changes require notice.

### Peer-to-peer (P2P) review
A conversation between the facility's/attending physician and the payer's
medical director to contest an adverse review determination.
**Why it matters:** Often the fastest way to reverse a concurrent-review
denial — but usually available only within a short window after the
determination. Know the window and who at the facility is responsible for
requesting it.

### Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage (NOMNC) / plan discharge notice
The advance notice a member must receive before covered SNF services end
(NOMNC applies to Medicare Advantage; other products have analogous notices).
**Why it matters:** Delivering payer-directed end-of-coverage notices late or
incorrectly can leave the facility holding non-covered days it cannot bill to
anyone. Know whether the contract assigns notice delivery to the facility and
on what timeline.

## Claims, denials & appeals

### Clean claim
A claim with no defect or missing required information, which the payer can
adjudicate without additional input.
**Why it matters:** Prompt-pay obligations and interest typically run only
from receipt of a *clean* claim — so the contract's clean-claim definition
controls when the payment clock starts. A vague or expansive definition lets
the payer restart the clock by requesting more information.

### Timely filing
The deadline for submitting a claim, measured from a stated trigger (date of
service, discharge, or — for coordination-of-benefits situations — the
primary payer's determination).
**Why it matters:** Timely filing denials are denials of otherwise payable
claims — pure write-off. SNF billing realities (Medicare-exhaust
determinations, retro-eligibility, payer-of-last-resort sequencing) make
short windows genuinely dangerous. Note the trigger date, not just the day
count, and any corrected-claim/resubmission window.

### Prompt pay
The payer's commitment (contractual and often statutory) to pay clean claims
within a stated number of days, sometimes with interest on late payment.
**Why it matters:** Determines cash-flow predictability. A contract silent on
prompt pay leaves only the state's prompt-pay statute — which may not cover
all product lines (self-funded plans are commonly outside state insurance
law).

### Denial
The payer's refusal to pay a claim or approve days — administrative (auth,
filing, format) or clinical (medical necessity, level).
**Why it matters:** Track denials by reason code per payer. Patterned
administrative denials are a process fix; patterned clinical denials on level
determinations are a contract-language problem to bring to the next
negotiation with data in hand.

### Appeal
The contractual process for contesting a denial, usually with defined levels
and a deadline for each.
**Why it matters:** Appeal deadlines are frequently short and strictly
enforced, and many contracts include waiver language — failing to appeal in
time forfeits the claim. Calendar the deadline the day the denial arrives.
Compare the SNF's appeal deadlines against the payer's own response
deadlines; asymmetry is a negotiation point.

### Recoupment / offset
The payer's recovery of alleged overpayments, often by deducting from future
remittances.
**Why it matters:** Look for notice requirements, a lookback limit, and a
dispute right *before* offset occurs. Unlimited-lookback, no-notice offset
language means any month's cash can shrink because of a claim paid years ago.

### Coordination of benefits (COB)
Rules determining payment order when a patient has multiple coverages.
**Why it matters:** COB determinations take time the timely-filing clock may
not allow. The contract should run filing deadlines for secondary claims from
the primary payer's determination date, not the date of service.

## Contract structure & network terms

### Evergreen clause (auto-renewal)
Automatic renewal for successive terms unless a party gives non-renewal
notice within a stated window.
**Why it matters:** Combined with no escalator, evergreen renewal quietly
locks in stale rates indefinitely. Calendar the non-renewal notice window —
it is the only recurring moment of negotiating leverage the contract gives
the facility.

### Unilateral amendment
A clause letting the payer change terms (rates, policies, manuals) by notice,
with continued participation deemed acceptance.
**Why it matters:** The contract signed is not the contract lived under.
Watch especially for incorporation of the "provider manual, as amended" —
a document the payer rewrites at will. Negotiation asks: mutual written
amendment, or a termination right exercisable within the amendment-notice
window.

### Termination — with cause / without cause
The grounds and notice periods for ending the contract; "without cause"
termination requires only notice.
**Why it matters:** Check symmetry (can both parties terminate without cause,
with equal notice?) and the *continuity-of-care tail* — the obligation to
keep serving current patients at contract rates after termination, and for
how long.

### Silent PPO / rental network
Arrangements letting entities other than the contracting payer access the
contract's discounted rates — via network-leasing clauses or "affiliates and
other payers" definitions.
**Why it matters:** The facility grants its negotiated discount to payers it
never negotiated with and gets no volume commitment in return. Look for
broad definitions of "Payer," network-rental language, and assignment
clauses. Ask: limit rate access to named entities, and require notice of any
entity claiming access.

### Most favored nation (MFN) clause
A clause requiring the facility to give this payer its best rate — if the SNF
contracts with anyone at a lower rate, this payer gets that rate too.
**Why it matters:** MFN clauses constrain every *future* negotiation and can
make a strategic discount to one partner contractually radioactive. Several
states restrict them; regardless, resist them.

### Letter of agreement (LOA) / single case agreement (SCA)
A one-patient (or short-term) contract negotiated when no participation
agreement exists — typically for an out-of-network admission the payer needs
placed.
**Why it matters:** The moment of maximum SNF leverage: the payer needs the
bed. Get the LOA signed *before* admission, and make it cover the full
per-diem terms plus carve-outs, authorization terms, timely filing, and
appeal rights — an LOA that states only a rate imports nothing else, leaving
every other term ambiguous. LOA experience with a payer is also useful data
when a full contract is later negotiated.

### Delegated entity / risk-bearing intermediary (management services organization, IPA, convener)
An organization standing between the payer and the facility — holding the
network contract, performing UM, or bearing risk (common in Medicare
Advantage and institutional special needs plan arrangements).
**Why it matters:** The entity on the contract's signature line determines
who authorizes, who pays, and who to appeal to — which may not be the health
plan whose card the patient carries. Confirm who bears insolvency risk: if
the intermediary fails, does the plan stand behind its obligations?

### Credentialing
The payer's process of verifying the facility's licensure, certification, and
standing before (and periodically during) participation.
**Why it matters:** Contracts often make payment contingent on completed
credentialing — admissions accepted before the credentialing effective date
may be treated as out-of-network. Confirm the effective date in writing
before taking the first patient.

### Provider manual (incorporated by reference)
The payer's operating manual — billing rules, UM procedures, documentation
requirements — which the contract incorporates as binding.
**Why it matters:** It is part of the contract but changes without a
signature. Obtain the current version before signing, and seek notice
requirements for material changes (this pairs with the unilateral-amendment
issue above).

### Lesser-of, carve-out, level
Cross-referenced above — the three terms most worth mastering first, because
they drive the largest per-day revenue differences in SNF managed care.
